## 0. Gancho (0:00)
**[TELA]** As duas casas aos 6 anos, depois corte seco para os 60 e volta.

> Two kids bring home the same perfect score.
> One hears, "You're so smart."
> The other hears, "You worked hard on this."
> Same kid. Same score. One different sentence.
> Fifty years later, one of them is still learning new things.
> And the other is still afraid of being found out.
> This is the whole life, side by side.
> And somewhere in the middle, there's a real experiment that shows the exact moment the two paths split.

## 1. Age 6 — The sentence (0:35)
**[TELA]** Cartão AGE | 6. Cena das duas casas, a nota 10, os balões.

> Meet Sam. Sam is six.
> In the first home, every good grade gets the same answer. "You're so smart."
> It feels great. Sam glows.
> In the second home, Sam hears something slightly different. "You worked hard on this. Show me how you did it."
> That also feels great.
> Right now, you can't tell these two kids apart.
> But each of them just learned what a good grade means.
> In the first home, it means: I am smart.
> In the second home, it means: I did something that worked.
> One of those is a label. The other is a recipe.
> And labels have to be protected.

## 2. Age 10 — The experiment (1:35)
**[TELA]** Cartão AGE | 10. Depois o cartão creme THE STUDY (Mueller & Dweck, 1998), com os peões contando 67% e 8%.

> Now Sam is ten. And this next part is not a story.
> In nineteen ninety-eight, two psychologists at Columbia University, Claudia Mueller and Carol Dweck, ran this moment on real kids.
> One hundred and twenty-eight fifth graders. Each one got a set of puzzles, easy enough to do well.
> Then each kid heard one sentence.
> Some heard, "You must be smart at these problems."
> Others heard, "You must have worked hard at these problems."
> Then came a choice. An easy task that would make them look smart. Or a harder one they could learn from.
> Of the kids called smart, sixty-seven percent picked the task that made them look smart.
> Of the kids praised for working hard, ninety-two percent picked the one they could learn from.
> One sentence. That was the only difference.
> Then the researchers gave everyone problems that were much too hard. Everybody struggled.
> The "smart" kids enjoyed it less, gave up sooner, and decided they just weren't good at this.
> And when they went back to easy problems, they did worse than before. The hard-work group did better.
> Then came the strangest part.
> The kids were asked to write down their score for students at another school. No one would ever check.
> Thirteen percent of the hard-work group lied about their score.
> In the "smart" group, it was thirty-eight percent.
> They weren't bad kids. They were protecting a label.

## 3. Age 16 — The first hard class (3:25)
**[TELA]** Cartão AGE | 16 · THE FIRST HARD CLASS. A contagem HARD THINGS TRIED aparece no alto de cada casa.

> Sixteen. From here on, we're back to the story.
> No study has followed two kids like this for fifty years. But watch how the same pattern plays out.
> Both Sams sign up for the hardest math class in school. And for the first time, both of them fail a test.
> In the first home, Sam does the math nobody taught. If I were smart, this would be easy. It's not easy. So maybe I'm not.
> Sam drops the class. And tells everyone it was boring.
> In the second home, Sam is just as upset. But the question is different. What did I do wrong?
> Sam stays. Gets a C. Then a B.
> By the way, the pressure to protect that label starts earlier than you'd think.
> In twenty seventeen, researchers played a guessing game with three hundred preschoolers. Some were told, "You are so smart." Then the adult left the room.
> About sixty percent of the "smart" kids peeked at the answer. In the other groups, it was about forty.
> Some of them were three years old.

## 4. Age 30 — The safe job (4:55)
**[TELA]** Cartão AGE | 30. Cena do escritório: TURNED DOWN x ACCEPTED, contagem 1 x 7.

> Thirty. Both Sams are good at their jobs. Honestly, the first Sam might be better. Careful. Never makes a mistake.
> Then a bigger role opens up. New team, new skills, a real chance of failing in front of people.
> In the first home's Sam, an old voice wakes up. What if this is the one that proves I was never that smart?
> Sam turns it down. Says the timing isn't right.
> The second Sam is scared too. But scared sounds different in this head. I don't know how to do this yet.
> Yet. One small word.
> Sam takes the job. The first year is rough. The second year is not.
> Look at the count at the top of the screen. Hard things tried.
> It's not that one Sam is braver. One of them just never had anything to lose by trying.

## 5. Age 45 — The sentence comes back (6:05)
**[TELA]** Cartão AGE | 45. Agora cada Sam é o peão grande, com uma criança pequena segurando uma nota.

> Forty-five. Now both Sams have a kid of their own. And one evening, that kid walks in with a perfect score.
> The first Sam smiles and says the most loving thing they know. "You're so smart."
> It's not a mistake. It's a gift. It's the exact gift Sam was given.
> The second Sam says, "You worked hard on this. Show me how you did it."
> And the sentence starts its second lifetime.

## 6. Age 60 — Who's still learning (6:55)
**[TELA]** Cartão AGE | 60. Peões de óculos. Casa B com violão/caderno; Casa A com a prateleira de troféus antigos.

> Sixty. The second Sam is taking guitar lessons. And is terrible at it. And goes back every Tuesday.
> The first Sam has had a good life. A real one. But there's a short list of things Sam never tried. A language. A business. A hard conversation.
> Not because Sam couldn't. Because Sam might not have been great at it. And being great was the whole identity.
> Fifty years of protecting a compliment.

## 7. The whole life, side by side (7:30)
**[TELA]** Quadro-resumo com os peões crescendo, 6 → 16 → 30 → 60.

> Here's the whole life, side by side.
> Six. "You're so smart." "You worked hard."
> Sixteen. Drops the hard class. Fails, and signs up again.
> Thirty. Stays where it's safe. Takes the harder job.
> Sixty. Still wonders. Still learning.

## 8. The honest part (7:55)
**[TELA]** Cartão creme, três estudos empilhados (2013, 2019, 2015).

> Now, the honest part. Because a sentence is not a spell.
> When researchers filmed fifty-three families at home, the toddlers who heard more praise for effort were, five years later, more likely to believe they could get better at things. That's real.
> But in that same study, being praised as a person didn't predict the opposite. The damage wasn't automatic.
> And in twenty nineteen, a team in Edinburgh repeated the original experiment with over six hundred children. They didn't find the effect.
> Even Carol Dweck has said they may have put too much emphasis on sheer effort. Telling a kid "good try" when nothing is being learned is just a nicer empty sentence.
> So this isn't about banning a word. Saying "you're smart" once won't break anyone.
> It's about what a kid hears a thousand times, and what they come to believe a failure says about them.

## 9. It's never too late (8:45)
**[TELA]** As duas casas aos 60 se misturando: o âmbar entra no marinho e a emenda some.

> And here's the good news. That belief is not locked in at six. Or at sixty.
> If you're raising a kid, try three swaps.
> Instead of "you're so smart," try "show me how you did that."
> Instead of "you're a natural," try "that took practice."
> And when they fail, instead of "it's okay, you're still smart," try "what would you try differently?"
> And if you were the "smart" kid, you can say it to yourself. I don't know how to do this. Yet.
